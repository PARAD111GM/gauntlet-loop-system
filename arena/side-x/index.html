The head wired up here has one job: look back for the nearest noun and copy what it is. That job is not a rule anyone wrote — it falls out of the eight matrices. Click any word to make it the one doing the looking.

What you're looking at

One row per token, one column per dimension. This toy uses 8 named dimensions so you can read them. GPT-2 uses 768 unnamed ones learned from data, where "noun-ness" is some direction nobody labelled and most directions have no name at all.

The last column is position: 0.2 × index, added to the word's own vector exactly as a real model adds a positional embedding. Without it attention is blind to order — a bag of words. That one ramp is the only reason this head can prefer nearby things.

An unknown word becomes the zero vector, which is honestly what an un-embedded token is: nothing to match on. Type one into the box above and watch the weights go flat.

Why three

The same 8 numbers are read three ways by three different matrices. That asymmetry is the whole trick: a token can ask for one thing, advertise another, and deliver a third.

Row 1 of WQ fires on verb; row 1 of WK fires on noun and goes negative on determiners. So a verb's query and a noun's key line up in channel 1, and the same query actively repels "the". That is what "this head looks for nouns" means mechanically.

Channel 3 of WQ has no input weights at all — it is pure bias, a constant 1.0 that multiplies each key's position. That is how a dot product ends up encoding "prefer recent".

The sliders scale rows of WK: the content rows (1, 2, 4) and the position row (3). They are real edits to the matrix printed here — drag one and watch the grid change.

3 Score every pair with a dot product

how well does the query match each key?

The dot product is the whole comparison

Multiply the two vectors channel by channel, add up. Each term is one channel agreeing or disagreeing: "you want a noun (2.0) and I am one (2.0)" contributes +4.0; "you want a noun and I am a determiner (−0.8)" contributes −1.6. Nothing else happens. There is no similarity function and no lookup table — attention is this sum.

Hover a row to expand that pair's terms in the strip above and light it up everywhere else on the page.

Scores are unbounded and signed. The negative ones matter more than they look: they are how a head says not this one, and after softmax they are what makes the pattern sparse instead of mushy.

4 Divide, then forbid

the same scores on one axis: raw, scaled by 1/√dk, then masked

Why divide at all

A dot product of d channels grows like √d. Feed those raw sums straight to softmax and it saturates: one weight goes to ~1.0, the rest to ~0, and the gradient through them dies. Halving them here pulls the dots together into a range softmax can still be soft about.

The mask

A causal model must predict token i+1 from tokens ≤ i, so every score for a future key is set to −∞. Not "very small" — −∞, because e−∞=0 exactly, so the future contributes nothing rather than a little.

Untick the box and watch a word attend to something it has not read yet: the leak that makes a language model look brilliant on paper and generate nothing.

5 Turn scores into a budget

exponentiate, then normalise so the weights sum to exactly 1

Why exponentiate

Scores can be negative; probabilities cannot. es is positive, monotone, and turns differences into ratios: two tokens one point apart end up e ≈ 2.72× apart in weight whatever their absolute scores. Which is why subtracting the largest score first — done here, for numerical safety — changes nothing at all.

The result is a probability distribution over the tokens so far. Attention is not "how related are these two" — it is a fixed budget of 1.0 being divided up. Boosting one word necessarily starves another. Drag the sliders and watch the total refuse to move.

Averaging, not choosing

The head never picks a winner. It takes a weighted average of value vectors — a blur across everything it looked at, in proportion to the weights. Confident attention gives you something close to one token's value; flat attention gives you mush, which is exactly why a flat head carries almost no information.

The part usually left out

That average is not the answer to anything yet. WO projects it back to 8 dimensions and it is added to the query token's own vector. Attention writes: after this head runs, the verb carries facts about the noun it found, and the next layer reads them as if they had been there all along.

Stack 12 layers × 12 heads of this and you have GPT-2.

row = who is asking, column = who is answering, brightness = weight. Click any cell.

The signature

Nobody reads one row in practice. You read the whole triangle and recognise a shape: a bright diagonal is a head attending to itself, a bright first column is an attention sink parking spare weight on token 0, a bright sub-diagonal is a "previous token" head.

Two behaviours are visible here at once, and they come from the same matrices. Look at the rows listed below: those queries have a real noun-seeking channel, and they spike hard on one cell. Every other row has a query whose only live channel is the recency bias, so it produces a smooth ramp toward the present instead — a flat, uninformative row.

The hatched cells are the mask. In a real implementation they are computed and thrown away: the triangle is enforced, never stored.

Reading this off a real model

the same six steps, at the size they actually run

What is honest here. This head is hand-built, not trained: its eight matrices are printed above in full and the sliders edit them in place. Every embedding, dot product, mask, softmax, weighted sum and residual update on this page was computed in your browser from those matrices while you watched. Nothing is recorded, nothing is an image of a result, and there is no network connection. The arithmetic is the same arithmetic.

What is simplified. dmodel=8 and dk=4 instead of 768 and 64; one head instead of 144; named dimensions instead of learned directions; a linear position ramp instead of learned or rotary embeddings; no layer-norm, no dropout, no multi-head concatenation, no training. None of that changes the six steps — it changes how big the numbers get and how impossible they are to name.
