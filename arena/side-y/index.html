Attention

A transformer is built out of one small idea repeated a few thousand times. This is that idea, one head of it, with every number on the table.

Interactive essayDrag anything that moves

Read this sentence slowly: the cat sat on the mat because it was warm.

When you reached the word it, something happened that you probably did not notice. You went back. Not to the beginning — you went back to a specific earlier word, and you brought it forward, and only then did the rest of the sentence make sense. The word it carries almost no meaning on its own. Nearly all of its meaning is borrowed.

That borrowing is the problem attention solves. A neural network reading text starts by turning every word into a list of numbers — a vector — and those vectors are computed one word at a time, in isolation. The vector for it knows everything about the word it in general and nothing whatsoever about this cat, this mat, or this particular warmth. Some part of the machine has to go and fetch. Attention is the part that goes and fetches.

This essay is about exactly one attention head. Not a whole transformer: no stacked layers, no feed-forward blocks, no normalisation, no training. One head, one sentence, and every intermediate quantity visible and adjustable. By the end you will have built a working head by hand and watched it learn nothing at all, because you will have set its weights yourself.

Everything below that looks draggable is draggable. Everything that looks like a number is a real number, computed live from what you are doing.

1Alignment is a number

Before any of the machinery, one piece of arithmetic. Attention is built almost entirely out of a single operation on vectors, and if that operation is comfortable, the rest is packaging.

Take two vectors, a and b. Multiply them component by component and add up the results. That is the dot product:

a · b  =  a1b1 + a2b2 + … + adbd  =  |a| |b| cos θ

The second form is the one worth carrying around. The dot product is large when the two vectors point the same way, zero when they are perpendicular, and negative when they point apart — scaled, in every case, by how long they are. It is a single number that answers the question how much do these two agree?

Two properties will matter later. First, the dot product is symmetric: a · b and b · a are the same number. Second, it is unbounded — make the arrows twice as long and the score quadruples. Both of these will need fixing.

2One question, many answers

Now put words in the picture. Give every word in our sentence a vector. Words that behave alike get vectors that point in similar directions; that is the whole premise of an embedding, and for the moment we can simply assert it and draw it.

Then take one more vector and call it the query: the thing doing the asking. Compare it against every word vector — every key — with a dot product, and you get one number per word. A ranking. A profile of relevance.

This is already recognisably attention: one vector interrogating a set of others and receiving a score for each. What is missing is any reason for the query to point somewhere useful, and any way for the scores to become an answer rather than a list.

3Queries and keys are different views of the same word

The obvious thing to try is to use a word's own embedding as its query, and every other word's embedding as its key. It fails, and the way it fails is instructive.

Under that scheme, the score between two words is the dot product of their embeddings, which measures similarity. But it is not similar to mat. A pronoun and a noun are about as unalike as two words can be, and yet the pronoun urgently needs the noun. The relation we want to detect — this is what that refers to — is not similarity. Worse, similarity is symmetric: if it attends to mat, then mat must attend equally to it, which is nonsense. Relations between words have direction.

So the head does not use the embedding directly. It keeps two learned matrices, WQ and WK, and projects each word through them — twice, into two different spaces:

qi = WQ ei      kj = WK ej two lenses on the same embedding

A matrix acting on a vector is a linear map: it stretches space along some directions, squashes it along others, and rotates the whole thing. It is worth seeing that rather than being told it.

Now write out the score between word i asking and word j answering:

sij = qi · kj = (WQei) · (WKej) = ei⊤ WQ⊤WK ej

Two things fall out of that last form. The matrices only ever appear as the single product WQ⊤WK — splitting it in two is a matter of parameter count, not expressiveness. And that product need not be symmetric, so sij need not equal sji. The head can look for a relation that runs one way. it may seek mat without mat seeking it.

Notice also that the projections are usually to a smaller space: a model with 768-dimensional embeddings might give each head only 64 dimensions to work with. A head is not asked to understand the sentence. It is asked to notice one kind of thing about it, cheaply, so that a dozen of its colleagues can notice a dozen others in parallel.

4Dot products grow with dimension

There is a nuisance hiding in that projection. Real heads work in 64 or 128 dimensions, not two, and a dot product is a sum over dimensions. Add more dimensions and you add more terms to the sum.

Suppose the components of the query and key are independent, with mean zero and variance one. Each product term then has variance one, and since the terms are independent, the variances add. The dot product of two d-dimensional vectors has variance d, which is to say a typical magnitude of √d . In 64 dimensions the scores are eight times larger than in one dimension, for no reason anyone intended.

Hence the 1 / √dk  that decorates every attention formula. It is not a deep idea; it is a unit conversion, chosen so that the numbers arriving at the next step have a sane size regardless of how many dimensions the head was given. Why a sane size matters is the subject of the next step, which is where the scores stop being scores.

5From scores to weights

We have a list of numbers, one per word, of arbitrary sign and arbitrary size. We want a set of weights: all positive, summing to one, so that the head's output is an average and not an explosion. The standard instrument is the softmax.

αj = exp(sj)∑m exp(sm) exponentiate, then normalise

Exponentiating makes everything positive; dividing by the total makes it sum to one. What makes softmax interesting rather than merely convenient is what exponentiation does to gaps. Only the differences between scores survive — add ten to every score and nothing changes — and a difference of 1 becomes a ratio of e ≈ 2.72, a difference of 5 becomes a ratio of about 148.

That temperature knob is the same knob as 1 / √dk  from the previous section. Now the reason for it is visible: let the raw scores drift to a spread of ±20 and the softmax pins itself to a single word. Its output stops responding to small changes in the input, which means the gradient reaching WQ and WK during training goes to nearly zero, and the head stops learning. The scale factor keeps the softmax in the part of its range where it still has opinions that can change.

6What actually gets fetched

The weights say where to look. They say nothing about what to bring back. It would be a mistake to bring back the keys: keys are addresses, shaped for being matched against. So there is a third projection, and a third vector per word — the value:

vj = WV ej      oi = ∑j αij vj the output is a weighted average of values

Address and cargo are separated. A word can be easy to find for one reason and worth retrieving for a completely different one — the key can encode I am a noun near the start while the value encodes warm, soft, furniture.

The convex hull in that figure is a real constraint and a real limitation. With weights that are positive and sum to one, the output is always some blend of what the values already contain. A single head is a routing device. Whatever nonlinear transformation the information needs, it will get elsewhere in the layer.

7Every word asks at once

So far one query. But every word in the sentence is simultaneously a query, a key, and a value — every word is asking its own question while answering everyone else's. Stack the queries as rows of a matrix Q, the keys as rows of K, and one matrix product produces every score at once:

S = QK⊤√dk  row i, column j: how much word i wants word j

Softmax is then applied along each row independently — every word's weights must sum to one on their own — and the result is the attention matrix. It is the most-looked-at object in machine learning, and it is worth being precise about how to read it: rows are askers, columns are answerers, and rows sum to one.

The mask exists because of how these models are trained. A language model predicts the next token, and it is trained on every position of a sentence in parallel — hundreds of predictions from one forward pass. That only works if the prediction at position 5 cannot see position 6, which would otherwise be a very short path to a perfect score and a useless model. The mask enforces the arrow of time inside the matrix multiply, at no cost.

8The whole thing, in one line

Every piece is now on the table, and they assemble into a formula you have probably seen before:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK⊤√dk ) V
Q — queries
what each word is looking for
K — keys
what each word offers as a handle
V — values
what each word hands over if chosen
softmax
turns a row of scores into a budget of attention that sums to one

9A head you can build by hand

Reading the formula is not the same as believing that a head can do anything. So let us build one, small enough to see completely, and give you its weights.

Our sentence is seven tokens: the cat sat on the warm mat. Each embedding will have four dimensions, split into two halves that we get to choose the meaning of.

  • The first two dimensions are content: a unit vector whose direction encodes what the word means. Repeated words share a direction exactly; related words sit near each other.
  • The last two dimensions are position: the token's index t written as a point on a circle, (cos tΔ, sin tΔ). Each successive token is one fixed step further around; we will use a step of Δ = 45°.

Now choose the weights. Let WK be the identity — keys are just the embeddings. Let WQ do two things: scale the content half by a gain gc, and scale and rotate the position half by a gain gp and an angle φ. That is a perfectly ordinary 4×4 matrix; it is just one we can describe in three knobs.

Because the two halves are orthogonal, the dot product splits cleanly into two independent terms, and the position term collapses through the cosine angle-difference identity into something remarkably simple:

sij = gc (ci · cj)  +  gp cos((i − j)Δ + φ)√dk  content agreement  +  relative position, over the usual scale

Read that second term carefully, because it is the interesting one. It depends on i − j — the distance between the two tokens — and not on where either one sits in the sentence. And it is maximised when (i − j)Δ + φ = 0. Set the rotation φ = −Δ and every token's strongest score lands on the token exactly one step behind it. A single angle in a single matrix, and the head has become a previous-token head.

Nothing here is a metaphor for what real models do. Rotary position embeddings, used in most current large language models, encode position as exactly this kind of rotation, in pairs of dimensions, at many different frequencies at once; the dot product of two rotated vectors then depends only on their relative offset, for exactly the reason above. Previous-token heads are among the first things found when researchers open up a trained transformer and look, and they are built out of precisely this trick.

10One head is not much, on purpose

Take stock of what the head in Figure 9 can and cannot do. It computes one bilinear form and one weighted average. It cannot represent and: it cannot say attend to the noun that is also to my left unless that conjunction happens to be expressible as a single dot product. Give it two relations to track and it will blur them together, splitting its weight between them and returning an average of two answers, which is usually worse than either.

So the design does the obvious thing. Run several heads side by side on the same input, each with its own small WQ, WK, WV, concatenate their outputs, and pass the result through one more matrix. Each head gets to be narrow-minded. One tracks the previous token, one matches repeated words, one carries the subject of the sentence forward — and because they read from and write to the same shared vector for each token, a head in layer 4 can pose a question that only makes sense because a head in layer 2 already answered a different one.

That composition is where the interesting behaviour lives. The famous example is the induction head: a pair of heads across two layers that between them implement the last time I saw this token, what came next? Predict that. The first head writes each token's identity into the position that follows it. The second head, sitting at the current token, looks for an earlier position whose key now carries that same identity — which is precisely the position just after the last time this token appeared — and copies forward what it finds there. Neither half is intelligent. The pair completes patterns it has never seen.

None of the weights in this essay were learned. Every matrix here was chosen by hand so that its effect would be visible, which is a luxury real heads do not get — theirs are found by gradient descent, and are correspondingly messier, more entangled, and better. But the machinery is the machinery. A trained head does what the head in Figure 9 does: it projects, it takes dot products, it divides by a square root, it exponentiates and normalises, and it averages. Everything else a transformer knows is built out of doing that a few thousand times.
